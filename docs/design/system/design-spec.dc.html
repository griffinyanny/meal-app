00 · Read this first

The six laws

If you only remember six things, remember these. Every specific rule further down is a consequence of one of them.

01

Light enters once, from above, and it is always gold.

One radial wash per screen, originating off-canvas at the top. It sits under the content layer and never moves or animates. No second light source, no cream wash, no second hue — a cream wash is the single most common drift in the current files and it flattens the whole palette.

02

Gold is who the app is. Cream is what you press.

Gold is reserved for the chef's presence — the orb, the live dot, the active tab, the chef's own voice. Cream is the hand: every filled button, every link, every interactive accent. The two never appear inside the same control, and their roles are never swapped.

03

Nothing you read twice is accent-coloured.

Titles, body, metadata, labels: cream-white through warm grey, always. The only coloured type in the product is the chef's italic rationale and the YOUR CHEF byline that introduces it. If a user will read a string more than once, it is neutral.

04

No cool white. Ever.

Every neutral — every border, every scrim, every low-alpha fill — is warm. rgba(255,255,255,x) reads blue against this floor and is the reason warm-dark UI looks cheap. Substitute rgba(240,222,190,x) at the same alpha, everywhere, with no exceptions.

05

A label is type. A control is a surface.

Nothing decorative wears a pill. If an element has a fill and a border, it must respond to a tap. If it is only naming something, it is flat uppercase type with no container.

06

Count the accents. Three gold in the content, one cream, one semantic.

Per viewport: at most three gold marks in the content layer, exactly one filled cream button, and at most one semantic hue. The active tab sits outside the count — persistent chrome is a constant, not an accent, and the eye stops reading it as one within a session. If you cannot make a screen legible inside that budget, the hierarchy is wrong — not the budget.

02 · The chef

The orb

The single most important element in the product, and until now the only one with no specification — it existed as five stacked layers inside the palette explorations and got redrawn slightly differently every time it appeared. This section is the definition.

It is an ember, not a chat avatar. Lit from within, cooling at the rim, breathing. Everything the app knows about the person using it is represented by this one object, so it never shares a screen with a second instance of itself.

The mark — the toque

An earlier draft of this spec removed the toque on the grounds that a mark on the light contradicts "light is the chef". That was wrong on both counts. A silhouette in light is still light — what would actually break the rule is a mark that covers the hot spot, which this one is drawn to avoid. And a bare glowing sphere is the single most generic form in the category; every assistant has one. The toque is the cheapest possible thing that makes this one specific, and it costs nothing structurally.

How it is drawn, so it stays a character and not clipart: a hairline stroke, never a fill — a filled toque is a sticker, a line drawing is a silhouette seen through flame. Warm near-black #2A1C04 at 85% opacity, 1.6 stroke on a 24 viewBox. Sized to 41% of the sphere and seated just below centre, so the highlight stays clear above the brim and the light still reads as coming from up-left.

Threshold: 40px and above. Below that the stroke goes sub-pixel and reads as dirt, so the header and inline orbs stay clean spheres. This is a lower bar than the ring and steam, which need 64px — a line survives shrinking better than a four-pixel wisp does.

Two thresholds. Ring and steam at 64px and above — below that a wisp is four pixels and reads as an artifact. The mark at 40px and above — a hairline survives shrinking better than a wisp does. Below 40px the orb is a clean sphere. These four sizes and no others.

Four states

Breath rate is internal activity. The ring is output. Between them the orb can say everything a status label would, without any text. Shown at profile size, 64px; steam is omitted here so the breath and the ring read cleanly.

Idle

ember 4s. Present, attending, asking for nothing.

Thinking

ember 1.6s, shallower travel, ring suppressed. Faster breath, nothing leaving the object.

Speaking

ember 4s + ring 3.4s. The only state where the ring runs continuously.

Resolved

Breath stops on the up-beat and holds at full. One ring fires and clears. Returns to idle once the user acts.

One orb per screen. Never two.

The chef is one entity, not a thread of messages. A second orb — for a second suggestion, a second card — turns a character into a bullet point.

The orb is not a button.

Nothing happens when you press presence. If the chef needs to be reachable, that is a cream control near it — never a tap target on the sphere itself.

No progress ring, no second hue.

Loading is the thinking state, not an arc sweeping the rim. The orb never turns green to confirm or red to fail — semantics live in the content, never on the chef.

03 · Lighting

Where the light comes from

This was the most inconsistent thing in the build. Fourteen screens carried fourteen different washes — six opacities, five origins, and two of them cream instead of gold. Resolved to three named recipes, chosen by what the screen is for.

The wash renders as an absolutely-positioned layer at inset:0; z-index:0; pointer-events:none directly inside the screen, beneath the scroll layer. It is never applied to a card, never animated, and never stacked with a second hue.

Don't

Give a card its own gradient, add a cream wash, animate the light, or place a second wash near the bottom of a list screen. Bottom-up light with no object to justify it reads as a rendering error.

04 · Surfaces

The elevation ladder

Six levels, and a surface may only ever sit one step above its parent. A card inside a card inside a sheet is how this palette turns to mud — if you need a third nesting level, the screen needs restructuring instead.

L0 · Floor

background:#0F0B08

The screen itself. Warm near-black, not espresso — dark enough that gold reads as light rather than as paint.

L1 · Glass card

background:rgba(70,58,46,.4)

border:1px solid rgba(240,222,190,.11)

The workhorse. Recipe rows, plan cards, list groups. Saturate stays low at 115% — push it higher and the warm glass picks up a blue cast from the floor.

A region inside a glass card. No blur of its own — it borrows the parent's. This replaces every rgba(255,255,255,.04) in the current build.

05 · Type

Hierarchy, filled in

The build had a 32px screen title and an 11px section label and nothing between them. Any screen with real internal structure — a recipe detail, a settings group, a card with a heading — had no level to reach for, so subsections were built out of body text at random weights.

Three levels added: Spoken headline at 26px, Group title at 19px and Row title at 15.5px. The ladder is now continuous, and every step changes both size and weight so hierarchy survives at a glance. A third level, the 26px spoken headline, was added once Onboarding and Reflect showed that a chef question filling a whole screen needs more than the 22px feature line.

Never skip two levels inside one container

A card headed with a Group title contains Row titles and body — not a second 32px heading, and not meta text pretending to be a heading.

Weight carries hierarchy, size confirms it

700 → 650 → 600 → 400 descending. Two adjacent levels never share a weight, so the ladder still reads when text is truncated to one line.

Tracking is a function of size

Negative above 19px (−.2 to −.5), zero from 12.5–15.5, positive below 11 (1.3–2px). No exceptions — this is what makes the type feel drawn rather than defaulted.

The Dinner vs Swap rule

The single most common error in the build: naming things with pills. "DINNER" is a label — it tells you what the slot is. "Swap" is a control — it does something. When both wear the same rounded container, the user cannot tell what is tappable, and taps the label.

07 · Navigation

The bottom edge

You asked whether the bottom bar is the right thing. Yes — the four-tab bar stays. Four flat destinations match the actual mental model (plan a week, find a recipe, shop for it, tune the chef), none of them nests inside another, and users move between them constantly rather than in sequence. A drawer or a hub screen would add a step to the app's most repeated motion.

What is wrong is everything around it. Three fixes below.

Why gold on the active tab, when gold is the chef

Because the tab bar is the one piece of chrome that says where you are inside the chef's world — it is presence, not action. You never press the tab you are already on. It is also why the tab is exempt from the accent budget in law 06 — it is always there, so it stops being an accent.

Rules

One per screen. If the chef can be told something here, this is the only place it happens. Suggestion chips may sit above it — they are shortcuts into the same field, not a second input.

Never mic-only, never text-only. Both affordances are visible at rest. A mic row with example text underneath is not this control.

Search is not this control. Searching is not talking — it stays in header pattern B, with a muted magnifier leading and its own mic trailing.

It is a control, not chrome. Inside a sheet it sits at the bottom of the sheet. On a screen it sits in content flow — it never floats above the tab bar, because that slot belongs to the primary action.

11 · Metrics

Geometry, spacing, motion

Radius in the current build lands wherever it was typed — 13px icon buttons, 20px cards, 11px pills, 7px chips — with no relationship to element size. A 7px radius on a 30px chip sitting beside a 20px radius on a 78px card is not a smaller corner; it is two different products. Radius is a function of height. Here is the whole system.

Radius

7px

checkboxes, radios, toggles · 20–24px

9px

badges, counts, micro-pills · 26–30px

12px

chips, segmented inners, small icon buttons · 32–38px

14px

icon buttons, toasts, secondary + ghost buttons · 40–46px

16px

primary buttons, tall inputs, segmented tracks · 47–54px

18px

bottom action bar, list cards, inset groups

22px

feature cards, sheets

46px

the screen itself, only — shown at true radius, top corners

Eight rungs, no others. Controls under 26px — checkboxes, radios, toggle knobs — take the 7px floor. Above that, for anything control-sized (up to ~54px) the rung is height ÷ 3 rounded down to the scale — 36 → 12, 44 → 14, 48 → 16, 52 → 18. Containers pick from the 18 / 22 pair by role rather than by height. Nested surfaces step down one rung and never match: a 16px segmented track holds 12px segments, a 22px card holds 18px groups. Device bezel and screen corners follow the hardware, not this scale, and hairlines — the home indicator, a text caret, a divider — are capsules at half their own height rather than a rung.
